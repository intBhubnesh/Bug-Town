# Bug Town

Project health as a village. One building per module, and every open bug is a
skeleton besieging it — the size of the mob *is* the bug count, not a scaled
figure. A module under attack smokes. One below 30 health is on fire.

Phase 1 of the [Gamified Bug Ecosystem PRD](../gamified-bug-ecosystem-prd.md):
a **visualization layer on seeded data**, so the team can see what the game feels
like before any reporting flow or Jira sync exists.

```bash
npm run dev        # http://localhost:3000
npm run check      # self-checks for the game maths and the seeded town
npm run typecheck
npm run build
```

## Your character

On first load you get a one-screen profile form: your name, your role, and the
character that walks the village as you — with a live 3D preview of the one
currently selected, idling in the panel beside the chooser.

| Role | Suggested character |
|---|---|
| Engineering Manager | Knight |
| Product Manager | Mage |
| Full-stack Developer | Barbarian |
| App Developer | Rogue |
| Tester / QA | Hooded Rogue |

The role *suggests* a character and never locks it — pick another and it stays.

Each character carries kit, listed in `CHARACTER_GEAR` and keyed by character
rather than by role (the chooser lets anyone pick any character, and a Barbarian
has no spellbook to hold):

| Character | Carries |
|---|---|
| Knight | one-handed sword + badge shield |
| Mage | spellbook |
| Rogue | knife |
| Hooded Rogue | hand crossbow |
| Barbarian | one-handed axe + round shield |

One-handed kit on purpose: the generic `Idle` and `Walking_A` clips are posed
for it, so a two-handed axe ends up floating beside the hand.

## Inside a module

Click a building, then **Go inside**. The interior is a floor plan built from
the KayKit Dungeon pack — a clean 4-unit modular grid — generated by
`src/lib/interior.ts`:

- **One room per feature.** The room grows with its bug count, flies a banner in
  its health colour, carries its health tint on the floor, and has its name,
  health and bug count on a board above the door.
- **Its bugs are the skeletons standing in it**, facing the doorway.
- **Module-level bugs stand in the corridor**, not in any room. That's what
  module-level means: the PRD routes them to the module's `General` feature, so
  a feature named `General` renders as the hall rather than a room.
- **Your character walks in** at the corridor entrance, and when you take a bug
  on it walks into that room and fights that skeleton.
- **Furnished.** Furniture along the walls, clutter on the floor, and one chest
  per room — gold if the feature is healthy. The decorator is handed the
  skeleton positions and refuses to place anything inside one, on top of
  another prop, or in the doorway.
- **Furniture follows health.** A room at 50+ is kept: tables, shelves, a
  made bed, candles, a mounted sword. A room below 50 is wrecked: broken
  tables, rubble, toppled barrel stacks, a broken sword. So "this feature is
  in trouble" is legible from the furniture before you read a number.
- Rooms are lit by their torches, a soft overhead light and a hemisphere fill.
  The first pass used torches alone and the floors came out black — a dungeon
  kit is authored assuming you light it yourself.

Everything is generated from the data — no hand-placed rooms. `interior.check.ts`
asserts that rooms never overlap each other or the corridor, every room has
exactly one doorway, every bug stands inside its own room with clearance from
the walls, no two skeletons stand on each other, nothing is lost between a
module and its plan, and there is floor under everything. It caught a bug on its
first run: a single-bug room puts its skeleton exactly on the seam between four
floor tiles.

Scale note: the dungeon pack is ~2.6× the village — a dungeon wall is 4 units
where a village house is 1.3 — so indoors the characters render near their
native ~2 units instead of the 0.85 they use outside.

### Fighting a bug

Open a module and press **Take it on** against any bug. Your character breaks
off the tour, walks to the exact skeleton standing for that bug, squares up and
attacks it; the skeleton turns from the wall to face you and its ground marker
turns blue. **Stand down** returns you to the tour. The seeded teammates do the
same automatically for the bugs they have claimed — so a claim is visible as a
duel, not as a status field.

How you fight depends on your character (`CHARACTER_ATTACK`), and the reach is
the readable part: a Barbarian closes to 0.85 units and chops, a Hooded Rogue
holds 2.2 units back and works the crossbow, a Mage casts from 1.9.

### Completing a fix

The bug you've taken on appears as a card in the left column, wherever you are —
village or interior. It carries the live ₹ stack, the live multiplier, the
points you'd bank, and the golden-window countdown while it lasts. **Complete
the fix** and:

1. The skeleton plays `Death_C_Skeletons` and crumples.
2. Two seconds later its **skull is left where it stood**, tipped on its side
   under a quiet green ring, with a "✓ SOLVED by &lt;you&gt;" plaque above it.
3. The feature **heals**. Because health is derived and never stored, the roof
   may change colour, the smoke may stop and the fire may go out — all of it
   falls out of the bug no longer being open.

A running list of what you've solved sits under the card.

No skull prop exists in any of the three packs, so `Trophy.tsx` loads the model
the bug *was*, hides every mesh except the ones named `_Skull` / `_Head` /
`_Jaw` / `_Eyes`, measures what's left and drops it to the floor. Same art, same
atlas, no new asset.

**Solved bugs keep their slot** in the layout rather than being removed. Removing
one would re-pack the remaining skeletons and every other bug in the module
would jump — so a solved bug stays in `bugs`, drops out of `openBugs`, and its
skeleton is swapped for a skull in place. `seed.check.ts` asserts exactly that:
health rises, counts drop, the fire goes out when a module is cleared, and the
bug order is byte-for-byte unchanged.

Scores are still not awarded — there is no ledger to write to yet, and faking
XP would be the one lie in an app whose point is trustworthy numbers.

Your character isn't a decoration. It walks a tour of the live modules: picks
one, walks there, stands and looks at it for a few seconds, moves on. A spinning
beacon, a shaft of light and a nametag make it findable in a wide shot, the
**Follow** button locks the camera to it, and the card top-left always says
which module you're standing at and how healthy it is. Click your character
(in the village or on the card) for your profile.

No accounts in phase 1, so the profile lives in `localStorage` on your own
machine — `src/lib/profile.ts`, which validates on read because anything in
localStorage is user-editable. "Start over" in your panel clears it and brings
the form back.

## How it's built

| Choice | Why |
|---|---|
| Next.js 16 App Router, TypeScript | Turbopack is the default in 16; no config needed. |
| `@react-three/fiber` + `@react-three/drei` | three.js in React without hand-rolling a render loop. |
| Seeded TypeScript data | No database yet. Phase 1 is about seeing it, not storing it. |
| `localStorage` for your profile | No accounts yet. Same shape survives the move to real sign-in. |
| Skeletons for bugs | Reads as the project being attacked, which is what a bug is. Severity is the model, the weapon and the size. |
| Procedural fire and smoke | Additive cones and a flickering light. No particle system, no texture, and it looks right at this camera distance. |

### Health is never stored

`src/lib/seed.ts` holds modules, features, developers and bugs — nothing else.
Feature health is derived as `100 − Σ severity damage of its open bugs`, module
health is the average of its features, project health the average of its live
modules. So the village can never disagree with the bug list, and deleting a bug
heals the building it was on.

### Where the rules live

`src/lib/game.ts` is the whole economy as pure functions: the severity table,
the bounty stack, the golden-window multiplier, XP levels, health bands, HP
drain. Every number there is a `RuleConfig` candidate once there's a database.

`src/lib/game.check.ts` asserts them against the PRD, including its own worked
example — a High bug fixed an hour after report must earn 58 performance points,
and level 4 must land at exactly 600 XP. If those numbers drift, the village lies.

`src/lib/seed.check.ts` guards the town itself: referential integrity (a bug
pointing at a feature that doesn't exist just vanishes with no error), that
every asset path it will ask for exists on disk (a wrong path renders nothing
and logs nothing), and the shape of the demo — all three health bands in use,
exactly one burning module, at least one clean one, someone mid-fix, someone at
low HP, and a live golden window. It caught a duplicate Jira key on its first
run.

**Run `npm run check` after touching the maths or the seed.**

### Why the health spread is designed, not arbitrary

With the PRD's damage table a Critical costs a feature 20 health and a Low costs
2, and module health is the average of its features. A module therefore only
falls below 50 when it has Criticals sitting open — and a "General" feature with
one typo in it drags a module's average *up*. So the seed concentrates bugs:
Records carries 6 Criticals across 2 features and lands at 25, which is what
puts it on fire. Spreading the same 35 bugs evenly would paint the whole village
green and the showcase would show nothing.

## Assets

for commercial use, attribution appreciated but not required. Vendored into
`public/assets/` so the app has no asset CDN to depend on.

- [Medieval Hexagon Pack 1.0](https://github.com/KayKit-Game-Assets/KayKit-Medieval-Hexagon-Pack-1.0) — tiles, 18 buildings in 4 colourways plus neutral, nature and props → the village
- [Character Pack: Adventurers 1.0](https://github.com/KayKit-Game-Assets/KayKit-Character-Pack-Adventures-1.0) — 5 rigged characters, 76 clips each → the developers using the platform
- [Character Pack: Skeletons 1.0](https://github.com/KayKit-Game-Assets/KayKit-Character-Pack-Skeletons-1.0) — 4 skeletons, 52 clips each, plus loose weapons → the bugs
- [Dungeon Remastered 1.0](https://github.com/KayKit-Game-Assets/KayKit-Dungeon-Remastered-1.0) — modular 4-unit floors, walls, doorways, furniture and clutter → the rooms inside a module (51 of its 203 pieces are vendored, 2.3 MB)

Licences are kept alongside them in `public/assets/`.

### Three things the packs don't do for you

All handled in `src/components/useModel.ts` and `Attacker.tsx`:

1. Every character GLB ships its gear as **unskinned sibling nodes that are all
   visible by default** — render a Knight as-is and he holds two swords and four
   shields at once. `useCharacter` keeps cosmetics (hat, cape, hood, helmet),
   hides the rest, and switches back on exactly the nodes named in
   `CHARACTER_GEAR`.
2. **The bind-pose bounding box includes that gear**, so it can't be used to
   scale a character. Scale is measured from the skinned body meshes only
   (~2.2 units tall, feet at y = 0, consistent across all three packs).
3. **The skeletons carry no weapons at all** — their axes, blades and staves are
   separate models in `Assets/gltf/`. The rig ships `handslot.l` / `handslot.r`
   socket bones for exactly this, so `<Weapon>` looks the socket up by name and
   parents the weapon to it.

The packs are addressed *by node name* in both cases, and a name that doesn't
exist renders nothing and logs nothing. `seed.check.ts` therefore parses the
GLBs directly (`gltf-nodes.ts`, no 3D library) and asserts every gear name and
every `handslot.r` bone really exists.

### One ranking, two places

The notice board standing in the square and the Team panel in the HUD both call
`rankDevelopers` in `src/lib/leaderboard.ts`, so they cannot disagree about who
is winning. Switching board in the panel changes the board in the square too —
it's one piece of state.

Ties share a rank: two developers level on points are both second and the next
is fourth, marked `=` in the panel. Equal values order by name so the order is
stable between the server's prerender and the browser's first paint.
`leaderboard.check.ts` covers the tie arithmetic, all three boards, input-order
independence, and that ranking never mutates what it was handed.

The board itself is a canvas texture on a wooden frame — no font file to fetch,
no CDN, one draw call, redrawn only when the data or the board changes. It
carries the **podium only**, three rows; at town-furniture size that is all
that reads from the street. Clicking it opens the full standings as a popup —
every developer with their score as a bar, level, HP, streak, finds, fixes and
₹, plus you at the bottom marked unranked. Escape or a click outside closes it.

Its dimensions are **derived from the buildings**, in `buildings.ts` alongside a
measured `BUILDING_HEIGHT` table: 1.30 units overall, which is taller than the
0.85 villagers reading it and well under the 1.64 median building, so it never
looms. The first version was 2.6 — higher than every module building in town
except the castle. `citywall.check.ts` asserts it stays in that band, that its
face is landscape, and that where it stands is inside the wall, off the ring
road and on flat ground.

### The wall closes exactly

`wall_straight` is exactly 2.0 long on its X axis, so `src/lib/citywall.ts`
lays the wall out as a **regular 26-gon whose side length is the piece length**.
Pick the number of sides and the radius follows — the ring then closes with no
gaps to hide and no segments sunk into each other. Towers sit on six of the
corners and one side is swapped for `wall_straight_gate`, on the +Z side where
the camera starts.

Radius 8.296 to a corner, 8.236 to the middle of a side, which puts the whole
wall inside the flat clearing (8.6) and clear of the ring road (7.4).
`citywall.check.ts` asserts all of that: every side exactly one piece long,
every piece on the inradius and square to its own tangent, neighbours butting
together, nothing on a slope, and every building and the road inside it. It
caught the rotation being one side's worth off on the first run.

### The ground is generated, not tiled

The village used to stand on visible hexagon tiles. It now stands on one
continuous disc of rolling terrain generated in `src/lib/terrain.ts` — a polar
mesh with value-noise height, vertex-coloured grass, and the ring road worn
brown into it. Flat dead-on inside `CLEARING_RADIUS`, so every building and
villager still sits at y = 0 with no slope fitting, then rising into forested
hills that lift at the rim to hide the edge of the world.

The hex grid is still what *places* the buildings, and its spacing is measured
rather than guessed: `hex_grass.gltf` is 2.000 × 2.309, a pointy-top hexagon of
size 2/√3. See `src/lib/hex.ts`.

**Note on the Forest pack.** [KayKit Forest Nature](https://kaylousberg.itch.io/kaykit-forest)
is itch.io-only (not on GitHub like the other three) and its **modular terrain
pieces are EXTRA-tier, paid** — the free tier is trees, rocks, bushes and grass
only. So the ground here is generated, and the trees and rocks come from the
hexagon pack's own `decoration/nature`, which already ships free-standing
`tree_single_*`, `trees_*` groves and `rock_single_*`. Dropping the Forest pack
in later is a swap of `NATURE_KINDS` and the asset folder, nothing more.

Animation clips already map onto the PRD's game states, which is why these packs
were chosen: `Idle`, `Walking_A`, `PickUp` (claim), `Interact` (review),
`Cheer` (fix confirmed), `Lie_Idle` (HP at zero), and on the skeleton side
`2H_Melee_Attack_Chop` and friends (a bug attacking its module).

### Performance notes

Thirty-five skeletons at nine skinned meshes each is the heaviest thing here.
Two deliberate choices keep it cheap, both marked in the code:

- **Skeletons cast no shadows.** It would double the shadow pass for detail
  nobody reads from this camera height.
- **The attackers drive `THREE.AnimationMixer` directly** (`useClip`) rather
  than drei's `useAnimations`, which eagerly builds an `AnimationAction` for
  every clip in the file — 52 per skeleton, ~1,800 across the town, to play one
  swing each.
- **Building footprints are a measured table** (`buildings.ts`), not measured
  from the loaded model. The layout needs them before anything loads: a
  skeleton's position depends on its building's footprint, and a villager
  walking over to fight that skeleton needs to know where it stands.
  `seed.check.ts` reads the real bounds out of the glTF and fails if the table
  drifts by more than a millimetre.
- **Modular architecture and furniture are merged per source file**
  (`useMerged`). A floor plan is ~60 floor tiles, ~80 wall segments and ~16
  props; merging gives one draw call per *model*, not per copy.
- **The city wall is three meshes** — segments, gatehouse, towers — however
  many pieces it takes.
- **The whole forest is one mesh.** All thirteen nature models share one
  material and one atlas, and the forest never changes, so ~175 placements are
  merged into a single static geometry: one draw call instead of 175.

First load pulls about 35 MB of GLB, mostly animation data that each file
carries its own copy of. Fine over localhost or a LAN; it would want
draco/meshopt compression before this went anywhere real.

## What is deliberately not here yet

Phase 1 is a showcase. None of the following exists, and none of it is stubbed:

- Reporting, claiming, reviewing or fixing a bug — the village is read-only
- Any database, API route, or auth — your profile is localStorage only, and
  your own ₹/XP/performance are all zero because there is nothing to earn yet
- Jira sync, webhooks, reconciliation
- Push notifications, quiet hours, streak jobs
- Moving between the village and an interior is a hard cut, not a camera move
- Mobile app
